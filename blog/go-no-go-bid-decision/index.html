Go/no-go bid decision: a simple checklist for contractors

Written October 17, 2026 (ET). BuildWithBNZ publishes this page. This is general guidance, not legal advice. Have your lawyer review contract terms.

The short answer: A go/no-go bid decision is the choice to bid a job or pass on it, before you spend hours estimating. Use the same short checklist every time. Score each job on fit, risk, competition, capacity and profit. If it doesn't clear your pass mark, say no and move on. Saying no to the wrong jobs gives you time to win the right ones.

A few terms first:

  • An ITB (invitation to bid) or RFP (request for proposal) is the request to price a job.
  • A bid bond is a guarantee from a surety that you'll sign the contract if you win.
  • Liquidated damages (LDs) are a set daily charge if you finish late.
  • Retainage is money the owner holds back from each payment until the job is done.
  • Go/no-go and bid/no-bid mean the same thing.

Why a go/no-go decision matters

Every bid costs time. Someone reads the documents, does takeoff, chases sub quotes and builds the number. If you lose, that time is gone. A quick go/no-go review protects that time. It also makes the decision less about gut feel on a busy day.

The go/no-go bid decision checklist

Answer each question before anyone starts the estimate.

1. Fit

  • Is this the kind of work we do well?
  • Is the size in our range?
  • Is it in an area we can staff and supervise?

2. Owner or GC

  • Have we worked with them before? How did it go?
  • Do they pay on time?
  • Do they award on price alone, or on more than price?

3. Contract risk

  • Are there liquidated damages? How much per day?
  • What's the retainage, and when is it released?
  • Is a bid bond, performance bond or payment bond required?
  • Are there terms we'd normally push back on?

4. Competition

  • How many bidders are there?
  • Is there a favorite already?
  • Can we win on something other than lowest price?

5. Capacity

  • Do we have the people to estimate it by the due date?
  • If we win, do we have crews and a project manager free?
  • Can we get enough sub coverage in each trade?

6. Schedule

  • Is the bid due date realistic?
  • Does the job schedule fit our backlog (work already signed)?

7. Profit

  • Can we make our target margin at a price that wins?
  • What would make this job lose money?

A simple bid go/no-go template

Score each line from 1 (bad) to 5 (great). Add the scores.

Factor Score (1–5) Notes
Fit with our work
Owner or GC history
Contract risk (5 = low risk)
Competition (5 = few bidders)
Estimating capacity
Crew and PM capacity
Schedule fit
Expected margin
Total (out of 40)

Set your own pass mark, and write it at the top of the sheet. Some factors should be deal-breakers on their own. For example, you might pass on any job you can't staff, no matter the total.

Keep one more line at the bottom: "Why we said go or no-go." In six months, those notes will show you which kinds of jobs you win.

Go/no-go vs bid/no-bid

There's no real difference. Some companies call it bid/no-bid, others call it go/no-go. What matters is that you use the same questions every time and write down the answer.

Speeding up the review with AI

Reading a full bid package just to decide whether to bid takes time. Some estimators now use AI to sum up the documents first. In one r/estimators thread, an estimator says they use an AI chat tool to sum up RFP documents for go/no-go calls, and that it has become part of their routine (r/estimators thread). That's one person's experience, not a test. If you try it, check the summary against the actual documents, especially bonds, LDs and due dates.

FAQ

What is a go/no-go bid decision? The choice to bid a job or pass on it, made before estimating starts.

What is the difference between go/no-go and bid/no-bid? None. They mean the same thing.

What should a bid go/no-go template include? A short, scored list: fit, owner history, contract risk, competition, capacity, schedule and margin, plus a pass mark and your reason.

Who should make the go/no-go decision? Usually the owner or chief estimator. One person makes the final call.
