# Concrete estimating software vs a spreadsheet: how to choose

*Checked October 20, 2026 (ET). Prices come from each vendor's own site on that date. BuildWithBNZ publishes this page.*

**The short answer:** Concrete estimating software measures slabs, footings and walls from your plans and ties each one to the concrete, rebar, forms, waste and labor behind it. A concrete estimating spreadsheet can do the math, but you measure and type every number yourself. If you bid simple flatwork a few times a month, a good spreadsheet may be all you need. If you bid from full plan sets every week, software usually pays for the time it saves. Test either one on a job you already priced.

A few terms first:

- A **cubic yard** is 27 cubic feet. Ready-mix concrete is ordered and priced by the yard.
- A **takeoff** is the list of quantities you measure from the plans: square feet of slab, linear feet of footing, and so on.
- **Formwork** (or forms) is the temporary mold that holds wet concrete in shape until it sets.
- **Rebar** is steel reinforcing bar placed inside the concrete. It's usually priced by weight or by the bar.
- A **waste factor** is extra material you add for spillage, uneven ground and over-dig. It's usually written as a percent.
- An **assembly** is a saved recipe. For example, one "footing" assembly can hold the concrete, rebar, forms and labor for each foot of footing.

## What each one has to handle

Whether you use a spreadsheet or software, a concrete estimate needs the same pieces.

| Item | What you need to figure | Spreadsheet | Software |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slabs | Area times thickness, thickened edges, base stone, vapor barrier | You measure and type the area | Measured on the plan and linked to the slab assembly |
| Footings | Length and size of continuous footings, count of spread footings | You count and type each one | Measured or counted on the plan |
| Walls and piers | Height, length, thickness, openings | You do the math per wall | Measured once, priced by the assembly |
| Rebar | Bar size, spacing, laps, chairs | Your own formulas | Built into the assembly in most tools |
| Formwork | Contact area of forms, reuse | Your own formulas | Built into the assembly |
| Waste | A percent added to yards and materials | One cell, easy to forget | Set per assembly |
| Pump | Whether you need a pump truck, how many pours | A line you add by hand | Usually a line you add too |
| Labor | Crew hours to form, place and finish | Your own production rates | Your own rates, stored in the assembly |

Notice the last two rows. Pump and labor depend on your crew, your market and the site. No tool knows those for you. You load your own rates either way.

## Where a spreadsheet breaks

A concrete estimating template is a fine place to start. These are the spots where it tends to fail:

1. **Measuring.** The sheet does the math, but someone still has to measure every slab and footing off the plans by hand or in a PDF viewer, then retype the numbers.
2. **Drawing changes.** When a new revision comes out, you have to find what changed and fix each number yourself. It's easy to miss one footing.
3. **Broken formulas.** One person types over a formula cell, and the total is wrong with no warning.
4. **Version mix-ups.** "Bid_final_v3_REAL.xlsx" gets emailed around, and nobody is sure which copy is right.
5. **Missed items.** A sheet only reminds you of what's on it. Thickened edges, dowels or pump costs get left off if there's no row for them.
6. **Speed.** Busy bid weeks show how long manual takeoff really takes.

## What concrete estimating software should do

Use this checklist in every demo.

**1. Plan takeoff.** Can you upload PDF plans and measure areas, lengths and counts right on the sheet?

**2. Concrete assemblies.** Does it come with assemblies for slabs, footings, walls and piers? Can you edit them to match how you build?

**3. Yards, rebar and forms.** Does it figure cubic yards, rebar and form area from your measurements without extra formulas?

**4. Your waste and labor rates.** Can you set waste by item and load your own crew production rates?

**5. Plan changes.** Can it show what changed between drawing versions so you update only what moved?

**6. Output.** Does it make the report you need: a bid proposal, a material list or a schedule of values (a line-by-line price breakdown that general contractors often ask for)?

**7. Excel.** Can you still send numbers to a spreadsheet if your team likes to finish pricing there?

**8. Pricing model.** Is it per seat, per person or per computer? Monthly or yearly? Every new estimator may add a license.

## When a spreadsheet is fine

Software isn't always the right call. A concrete estimating spreadsheet or template can be enough when:

- You mostly pour driveways, patios and simple slabs, not full commercial plan sets.
- You bid a handful of jobs a month, and one person does all the estimating.
- Your jobs repeat, so the same rows and rates cover almost every bid.
- You already measure fast in a PDF viewer and just need the math.

If you stay with a sheet, protect it. Lock the formula cells. Keep one master copy. Add rows for pump, finishing, dowels and thickened edges so they never get skipped. Check the yards against a quick hand calculation on every bid.

## How to test concrete estimating software

1. **Pick a job you already priced.** You know the right answer.
2. **Run the full takeoff.** Slabs, footings, walls and piers, start to finish.
3. **Load your own numbers.** Your waste factor, mix price, rebar price and crew rates. Don't judge it on defaults.
4. **Compare.** Check yards, rebar, form area and labor hours against your spreadsheet.
5. **Swap in a revision.** See how long it takes to update when the drawings change.
6. **Ask about the term.** Monthly or yearly? Per seat or per computer? What happens if you cancel?

## FAQ

**Is a concrete estimating spreadsheet good enough?**
For simple flatwork you bid a few times a month, often yes. A spreadsheet starts to break when you bid from full plan sets, when drawings change between versions, or when more than one person edits the same file.

**What is the difference between a concrete estimating template and software?**
A template is a spreadsheet with the formulas already set up. You still measure the plans and type in every number. Software usually measures from the plans and links each measurement to the material, labor and waste behind it.

**How much does concrete estimating software cost?**
As of Oct 20, 2026, on the vendors' own pricing pages: PlanSwift is $2,000 or $3,000 a year for one seat. TradeTek, sold by Total Takeoffs, is $99 a month or $995 a year per computer. ScopeTakeoff is $100 per person per month.

**How do you figure cubic yards of concrete?**
Multiply length by width by thickness, all in feet, to get cubic feet. Then divide by 27, because there are 27 cubic feet in one cubic yard. Add your waste factor before you order.

**Should I test software on a job I already bid?**
Yes. Use a job you already priced so you know the right answer. Compare yards, rebar, form area and labor hours against your own numbers before you trust the tool.
